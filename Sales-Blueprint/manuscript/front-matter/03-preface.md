# Preface

I lost a deal in my second year of running my own company that I still think about. Six weeks of calls, a proposal I was proud of, a buyer who nodded at everything I said. Then silence. When I finally got the decision-maker on the phone, she told me they'd gone with a competitor who was 30 per cent more expensive. I asked why. She said, "Because we trusted they'd actually deliver."

I had a better product. I lost anyway. That is the moment this book started, even though it took me another nine years to write it down properly.

For most of my career I believed selling was a talent. Some people had the gift: the easy charm, the quick comeback, the knack for closing. I didn't have it, so I worked around it with research, preparation, and an uncomfortable amount of honesty with prospects about what my product couldn't do. It turned out that approach won more often than the charm did. Not because charm doesn't matter. Because trust matters more, and trust is not a personality trait. It is a sequence of specific, repeatable things a seller does, in a specific order, that most sales training skips straight past.

I spent the next decade testing that sequence, first on my own deals and later while advising other founders and small business owners who, like me, were good at their craft and uneasy about selling it. The pattern held up across an accounting firm, a landscaping company, a software team, and the office fit-out business you will meet in Module 1. Different products, different price points, same nine moments determining whether the deal closed.

That sequence became the Blueprint Framework: Build Trust, Learn the Customer, Understand the Problem, Establish Value, Present the Offer, Remove Doubt, Inspire Action, Nurture Relationships, Turn Customers into Advocates. Nine words, nine chapters, one system.

This book is not a collection of tricks. I have read plenty of those, and most of them share the same flaw: they describe what a great salesperson does without explaining why it works, so the reader can't adapt it when the situation changes. Every chapter in this book answers five questions: what the idea is, why it works, how to do it, what to do with it in the next twenty-four hours, and how to know if it worked. If a chapter can't answer all five, it didn't make the final draft.

You'll notice one company running through the whole book: Kestrel Office Interiors, a fictional office fit-out business founded by a woman named Priya Shah. I built her and her business from a composite of real conversations I've had with founders over the years, changed enough that no single client would recognise themselves in it, but close enough to reality that you'll recognise the situation. Watch what she does at the start of each chapter and what changes by the end of it. That is the framework in motion, not just in theory.

I still think about that six-week deal I lost. These days, though, it is a much rarer story. I hope that by the time you finish this book, it is a rarer one for you too.

Samuel S. Omobusuyi
