# Module 12: Digital Selling

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Adapt each stage of the Blueprint Framework to a deal that starts and often finishes without an in-person meeting
- Identify the digital equivalents of the trust signals covered in Module 3
- Run discovery and diagnosis well over video and asynchronous messages
- Recognise where digital selling changes the pace of the framework, without changing its order

## Kestrel Office Interiors: a lead that never set foot in the office

A year after the Bramble Analytics project, Priya published a short write-up of it on Kestrel's website: nothing salesy, just a description of the uneven-attendance problem and how the flexible-room design had solved it. Three weeks later, an email arrived from Tom, who ran operations for a fully distributed twelve-person startup renting a single shared floor two days a week. "We're basically the opposite of Bramble," he wrote. "Nobody's ever in the office at the same time, and we're paying for space nobody uses most days. Your write-up is the first thing I've read that describes our actual problem." Priya had never met Tom, and wouldn't meet him in person until the installation was nearly finished.

## The framework doesn't change; the signals do

Every stage from Module 3 through Module 11 still applies to a deal like Tom's. What changes is how each stage gets executed when a handshake, a walk around an existing office, or a face-to-face read of hesitation isn't available. Trust, discovery, diagnosis, value, offer, doubt, action, nurture, and advocacy are still the sequence. Digital selling is the same framework, run through a narrower set of channels, usually faster and with less room for the ordinary social cues that used to do some of the work automatically.

## Trust signals without a handshake

In Module 3, Priya's first competence signal with Dominic was largely conversational: what she said, how she listened. With Tom, the first trust signal happened before they'd spoken at all: the published write-up itself, which demonstrated specific, checkable competence (a real diagnosed problem, a real result) without requiring a meeting to prove it. Response speed matters more here too. A buyer who found you through a search or a recommendation and gets a same-day, specific reply forms a different impression than one who waits a week for a generic acknowledgement; the wait itself functions as a reliability signal, just a negative one. Priya replied to Tom within two hours, referencing the exact line in his email about paying for unused space.

## Discovery over video and asynchronous messages

Module 4's three questions (who else needs to be comfortable, what they're personally measured on, what would be hard to defend) work identically over video; what changes is how much you have to ask outright rather than read from the room. In person, a buyer's hesitation often shows in their posture or their pause before answering. Over video and especially over email, it often doesn't show at all unless you ask for it directly. Priya's discovery call with Tom included a question she rarely needed to ask Dominic in person: "Is there anything about this that you're not sure your co-founders will agree with?" Tom told her, unprompted, that one co-founder wanted to give up the office entirely, which reframed the whole diagnosis that followed.

## Presenting and deciding without being in the room

Module 7's fixed-scope offer and Module 8's specific-doubt resolution both translate directly to a digital process, usually as a shared document or a short recorded walkthrough rather than a page handed across a table. What doesn't translate well is a long, unstructured video call standing in for what should be a written, referenceable offer; a buyer reviewing a decision with people who weren't on the call needs something they can forward, not just a memory of what was said. Priya sent Tom a recorded video walkthrough of the proposed layout alongside the same fixed-scope document she'd have used in person, specifically so he could share it with his co-founders without needing her present to explain it.

## Apply this today

Look at your current inbound or remote-sourced leads. Pick one stage from Modules 3 to 8 and write down its specific digital equivalent for this deal: what proof, question, or document replaces the in-person version. Send or ask it within the next working day.

## Measure success

You'll know this is working when remote-sourced deals move through the framework at a comparable rate to in-person ones, rather than stalling for lack of the informal cues those meetings used to provide. A shrinking gap between your response time and the buyer's first message is an early, leading sign.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming digital deals need a different framework rather than digital equivalents of the same one
- Letting response time slip, which reads as a negative reliability signal before the conversation has even started
- Replacing a structured discovery question with silence, on the assumption a video call will surface hesitation on its own
- Using an unstructured call where a shareable, referenceable document is what the buyer's other stakeholders actually need

## Chapter summary

- The Blueprint Framework's stages and order don't change online; the channels and cues used to execute each stage do.
- Published proof of competence can build trust before a conversation even starts, and response speed itself acts as a reliability signal.
- Discovery questions that get read from body language in person need to be asked directly over video or async messages.
- Offers and doubt resolution should produce something shareable, not just something said, so absent stakeholders can review it themselves.

## Key takeaway

**Digital selling doesn't change the sequence; it removes the cues that used to carry some of the weight, so you have to carry it in words instead.**
