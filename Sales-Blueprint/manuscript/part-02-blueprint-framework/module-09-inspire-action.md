# Module 9: Inspire Action

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Explain why agreement and action are two different things, separated by ordinary friction rather than doubt
- Identify the real reason a convinced buyer hasn't yet acted
- Reduce the next step to the smallest possible action a buyer can take immediately
- Use genuine deadlines instead of manufactured urgency to prompt a decision

## Why I follows R

Marguerite told Priya, in as many words, that nothing was stopping the project. A week later, the proposal was still unsigned. That gap is not a doubt Module 8 missed; it's a different problem entirely. A convinced buyer and a committed buyer are not the same person, and the distance between them is usually filled with ordinary friction (a busy week, a task that hasn't reached the top of a list, a form that takes longer to review than expected) rather than anything Remove Doubt could have addressed.

Skip this stage, or simply wait for the buyer to act on their own momentum, and you get the failure mode that defines it: the seller waits for the buyer to chase them, and a genuinely won deal quietly stalls on an admin desk instead of closing.

## Kestrel Office Interiors: the unsigned proposal

Priya's old habit here would have been to wait, or to send a version of "just checking in, let me know if you have any questions," which asks the buyer to do more work rather than less. Instead, she called Dominic directly and asked plainly what was holding things up. "Honestly," he said, "Marguerite's completely convinced, but signing a formal proposal has become one more thing on a very long list. It's not a priority conversation for her; it's an admin task she hasn't gotten to."

That told Priya exactly what to do. She didn't need to resell the value or resolve another doubt. She needed to shrink the remaining task and give it a real, not invented, reason to move to the top of the list. She offered Marguerite a fifteen-minute call, not to discuss anything new, but specifically to walk through and sign, and she reminded Dominic, honestly, that the installation slot they'd built the whole schedule around would need to be released to another client if the agreement wasn't signed by the following Friday. Marguerite took the call. The agreement was signed before it ended.

## Agreement and action are separated by friction, not doubt

Once you've correctly diagnosed a doubt as resolved, as Marguerite's clearly was, don't keep treating the deal as if a doubt is still the obstacle. Ask instead what's physically or organisationally in the way of the buyer taking the next action: competing priorities, an unclear process for who actually signs, a document that's longer or more effortful to deal with than the decision itself deserves. Priya's fifteen-minute call worked because it replaced an open-ended task ("review and sign this document at some point") with a bounded, scheduled one, which is a much easier thing for a busy person to say yes to.

## Shrink the next step

Every stage before this one has been building toward a decision. This stage is about making that decision as small and specific an action as possible at the moment it's needed. "Sign this document" is a bigger, vaguer ask than "give me fifteen minutes to walk through it together and sign." The content of the decision doesn't change; the size of the immediate action does, and smaller, more specific actions get taken faster than large, open-ended ones.

## Genuine urgency, not manufactured urgency

The installation slot Priya mentioned to Dominic was real: Kestrel's schedule genuinely depended on it, and missing it genuinely would have pushed the project back. That's the difference between a real deadline and the manufactured kind ("only two spots left this month!") that buyers have learned to discount, and which actively damages the trust built all the way back in Module 3 if it's ever caught being untrue. Use a real constraint when one exists. Don't invent one when it doesn't.

## Apply this today

Look at any deal in your pipeline where the buyer has said something close to "yes, we want to do this" but hasn't yet acted. Call them and ask plainly what's in the way. Then reduce whatever they tell you into the smallest possible next action, scheduled specifically, rather than left open-ended.

## Measure success

You'll know this is working when the average time between a buyer's verbal agreement and a signed commitment shrinks, and when you're the one initiating that final push rather than waiting for the buyer to find time for it on their own. A deal that stalls for weeks after "we want to do this" is a sign this stage was skipped, not that the buyer changed their mind.

## Common mistakes

- Treating friction after agreement as if it were an unresolved doubt and reopening a conversation that's already settled
- Leaving the next action open-ended ("let me know when you're ready") instead of scheduled and specific
- Waiting passively for the buyer to act on their own momentum
- Inventing artificial urgency instead of using a genuine constraint, and risking the trust built in earlier stages

## Chapter summary

- A convinced buyer and a committed buyer are different people, usually separated by ordinary friction, not lingering doubt.
- Diagnosing that friction correctly (a priority problem, not a trust problem) tells you what kind of push is actually needed.
- Shrinking the next action to something small, specific, and scheduled gets it done faster than an open-ended request.
- Genuine deadlines prompt action without damaging trust; manufactured urgency risks the trust built in every earlier stage.

## Key takeaway

**A convinced buyer who hasn't acted doesn't need more persuading; they need a smaller next step.**
