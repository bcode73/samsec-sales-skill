# Module 4: Learn the Customer

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Explain why Learn the Customer only works once trust has been established, and not before
- Ask the three questions that reveal a stakeholder's role, pressure, and personal stake in the decision
- Map the full buying committee for a deal at the first opportunity, rather than discovering it late
- Apply a discovery structure that feels like genuine curiosity rather than an interrogation

## Why L follows B

Dominic answered Priya's question about the last supplier honestly, in detail, within minutes of meeting her. That doesn't happen by accident. A buyer who is still filtering you through suspicion gives you the polished, careful version of their situation, the one that reveals nothing you could use against them. Trust is what earns you the unpolished version, which is the only version worth learning from.

This is why Learn the Customer sits second, not first. Ask discovery questions before trust exists and you'll get answers, but not honest ones, and every stage built on top of them (the diagnosis in Module 5, the value case in Module 6) inherits that dishonesty without you realising it. Skip trust and go straight to discovery, and you end up doing exactly what cost Priya the accountancy job in Module 2: building a confident, detailed picture of the wrong thing.

## Kestrel Office Interiors: what Priya learned about Dominic

With the trust from their first meeting in place, Priya's second conversation with Dominic went somewhere her pitches rarely went before. She asked him directly: "Who else, beyond you, needs to be comfortable with this decision before it happens?" Dominic named two people without hesitating: Marguerite, the co-founder who controlled the budget, and a wider team who'd complain loudly if the new office disrupted a busy quarter. Priya asked what Dominic personally was measured on this year. "Keeping the team happy without blowing the budget my co-founder set," he said. "If this goes well, nobody notices. If it goes badly, everyone does, including her."

That single answer told Priya more than four meetings with Neil had told her at the accountancy firm. Dominic wasn't just the office manager; he was personally exposed if the project disrupted the team or ran over cost, and his co-founder was the one he had to answer to.

## Three questions that reveal what you actually need to know

Generic discovery ("tell me about your business," "what are your goals?") produces generic answers, because the question doesn't signal that you're going to use the answer for anything specific. Three sharper questions do more work in less time.

**"Who else needs to be comfortable with this before it happens?"** surfaces the buying committee immediately, rather than leaving you to discover it in a later meeting the way Priya did with Neil and Farah. **"What are you personally measured on this year?"** surfaces the stakeholder's actual stake in the outcome, which is usually more specific and more personal than "we want a nicer office." **"What would make this decision hard to defend to your board or your team?"** surfaces the objection you'll need to resolve later, long before Module 8 asks you to name it.

None of these questions work as a checklist read aloud in order. They work because trust, built in the previous stage, makes the buyer willing to answer them honestly, in a normal conversation rather than a form.

## Learning the organisation, not just the person

Individual discovery tells you about Dominic. Organisational discovery tells you how Bramble Analytics actually makes this kind of decision: whether it needs board sign-off, whether there's a formal procurement process, how fast decisions have moved on similar purchases before. Priya asked Dominic how the company had chosen its accounting software the previous year, a purchase of comparable weight but nothing to do with office design, specifically to learn the shape of their decision process rather than the details of this particular deal. He told her it took six weeks and needed both his and Marguerite's sign-off, which set her expectations for this sale's timeline before she'd built a single proposal.

## Apply this today

In your next conversation with an existing prospect, ask one question you haven't asked yet: what they're personally measured on this year, or who else needs to be comfortable with the decision. Write down the answer immediately, in their words, not your summary of it.

## Measure success

You'll know this is working when your proposals start referencing details a generic pitch never would: a specific internal pressure, a named co-decision-maker, a comparable past purchase and how long it took. A rising number of deals where you can name the full buying committee before the second meeting, rather than discovering a new name in the fifth one, is the concrete sign this stage is doing its job.

## Common mistakes

- Running discovery before trust is established, and getting the polished answer instead of the real one
- Asking broad, generic questions that produce broad, generic answers
- Learning about the individual in the room without learning how their organisation actually decides
- Treating discovery as a one-time form to fill in rather than an ongoing habit throughout the deal

## Chapter summary

- Learn the Customer only works once Build Trust has earned the buyer's honest answers, which is why it comes second.
- Three specific questions (who else must be comfortable, what they're personally measured on, what would be hard to defend) reveal more than generic discovery ever will.
- Learning the organisation's decision process, not just the individual's preferences, sets realistic expectations for the whole deal.
- Discovery is not a one-time form; it continues as new stakeholders and new information appear.

## Key takeaway

**You cannot diagnose a problem you never asked the right questions about.**
