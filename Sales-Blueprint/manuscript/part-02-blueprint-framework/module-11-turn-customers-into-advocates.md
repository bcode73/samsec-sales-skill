# Module 11: Turn Customers into Advocates

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Explain why hoping for referrals produces far fewer of them than asking for referrals deliberately
- Identify the moment a satisfied customer is most ready to advocate for you
- Make a specific, low-effort referral ask instead of a vague, easy-to-forget one
- Ask for something valuable even when the customer has no specific introduction in mind
- Close the loop with an advocate so the relationship compounds instead of quietly ending
- Recognise when a well-meaning advocate has been asked too often

## Why T follows N

Dominic's comment in Module 10, that other teams at Bramble Analytics had started asking about the flexible rooms, was exactly the kind of moment Priya's old habits would have let pass by. She'd have been pleased to hear it, said something polite, and moved on, quietly hoping it might turn into something later. Turn Customers into Advocates is what happens instead: treating that comment as a specific, actionable opening rather than a nice thing someone said.

Skip this stage, or treat referrals as something that happens to lucky businesses rather than something you build, and you get the failure mode that defines it: hoping for referrals instead of building a system that reliably produces them.

## Kestrel Office Interiors: the ask Priya used to avoid

In the same call where Dominic mentioned the interest from other teams, Priya asked a specific question instead of a vague one. Not "let us know if you hear of anyone who needs this," which is easy to agree to and easy to forget, but: "Would you be comfortable introducing me directly to whoever runs operations for those other teams? I'd rather hear it from you than send a cold email." Dominic said yes immediately and made the introduction that afternoon. Two weeks later, Priya sent him a short note: the introduction had led to a first meeting, and win or lose, she wanted him to know his name had opened the door.

Neither the ask nor the thank-you cost Priya anything beyond a moment's attention. Both of them were structured, not left to chance, and both would have been easy to skip.

Asking directly like this feels uncomfortable to a lot of sellers for the same reason Module 1's myths persist: it feels like imposing, the same way a direct opening question can feel like pressure to someone who's never separated genuine curiosity from pushiness. The discomfort is misleading in the same way. A satisfied customer who is asked plainly for a specific, easy favour is rarely bothered by it; the discomfort belongs almost entirely to the person asking, not the person being asked.

## The right moment is right after proof, not right after the sale

A customer's willingness to advocate for you peaks at the point they've seen tangible proof that what you promised actually happened, which is often weeks or months after the invoice was paid, not on the day it was signed. Dominic was more ready to make an introduction six weeks after installation, once he'd watched the space genuinely solve the problem it was built for, than he would have been on closing day, when the outcome was still a promise rather than a fact. This is why Turn Customers into Advocates sits after Nurture Relationships in the sequence: nurture is what puts you in the room at the moment proof actually lands.

## Ask specifically, not generically

"Let me know if you hear of anyone" asks the customer to do ongoing, unprompted work on your behalf, which is exactly the kind of open-ended task that quietly never happens, for the same reasons an unsigned proposal sits on a desk in Module 9. A specific ask, aimed at a specific person or a specific kind of introduction, asks for one bounded action instead. Priya's question named the exact people Dominic already had in mind and asked for one specific thing: an introduction, not a general endorsement.

Deliver a confirmed win, ask specifically at the moment proof lands, make the ask easy to say yes to, then close the loop, which is what makes the next revolution of the flywheel easier than the last.

## When there's no specific person to name

Not every satisfied customer has a specific introduction in mind, and asking "do you know anyone?" with nothing more specific than that reverts to the same vague, easy-to-forget ask this stage exists to avoid. In that situation, ask for something else concrete instead: permission to use their result as a named case study, a short written testimonial about the specific problem that got solved, or a public review describing the outcome in their own words. Each of these is a bounded, specific action just like an introduction is, and each one does real work even without a named lead attached to it: a detailed case study becomes evidence the next prospect can check, in exactly the way Module 3 describes.

## Close the loop, every time

Priya's short note back to Dominic (the introduction led to a meeting, and she wanted him to know) did more for the relationship than the referral itself. A customer who makes an introduction and never hears anything about it again learns, correctly, that the effort disappeared into a void, and is less likely to offer another one. Closing the loop, telling the advocate what happened, win or lose, is what turns a single referral into a habit rather than a one-off favour.

## Vague ask versus specific ask

| Situation | Vague ask (easy to forget) | Specific ask (bounded and actionable) |
|---|---|---|
| A named person comes to mind | "Let me know if you hear of anyone" | "Would you introduce me directly to this person?" |
| No specific person comes to mind | "Keep me in mind for anyone who needs this" | A named case study, testimonial, or review |
| After the introduction happens | Nothing further said | A short note closing the loop on what happened |

## The compounding value of an advocate

A customer who refers even one similar business roughly doubles the value Kestrel gets from the original relationship, without any proportional increase in Priya's own prospecting effort. Over several years and several introductions, that compounding effect is usually larger than the value of the original project itself.

## Recognising when you've asked too often

An advocate who has made two or three introductions for you has done something genuinely generous, and treating them as an inexhaustible source by asking again every time you need a new lead will eventually turn goodwill into fatigue. There's no fixed number that marks the line, but a useful check is whether your next ask is driven by this customer's own enthusiasm (they've mentioned something unprompted, the way Dominic did) or by your own need for pipeline. The first is a genuine opening. The second is the same pressure Module 9 warned against, aimed at a relationship instead of a single deal.

## Apply this today

Think of a customer who has recently told you something positive about the result you delivered, even in passing. Ask them one specific question: whether they'd be willing to introduce you to a particular person or a particular kind of contact, rather than a general request to keep you in mind.

## Attach the ask to your existing nurture cadence

Rather than waiting for a customer to volunteer something you can act on, build the referral ask into the six-week and six-month check-ins from Module 10. When proof has clearly landed and the conversation is already positive, ask the specific question then, instead of treating it as a separate task you have to remember to do at some other time. The nurture cadence already puts you in the room at the right moment; this just makes sure you use it.

## Measure success

You'll know this is working when a growing share of your new business arrives through named introductions rather than cold outreach, and when past customers respond to a specific referral ask more often than they ever responded to a general one. Consistently closing the loop with advocates, and seeing some of them refer more than once, is the sign the system is compounding rather than relying on luck.

## Common mistakes

- Asking generally ("let me know if you hear of anyone") instead of specifically
- Waiting for a referral to happen unprompted instead of asking at the moment proof has just landed
- Falling back on a vague ask when there's no specific introduction available, instead of requesting a case study, testimonial, or review
- Never closing the loop, so an advocate has no idea whether their introduction led to anything
- Treating a single referral as a nice surprise rather than the first cycle of a repeatable system
- Returning to the same advocate again and again out of your own need for pipeline rather than their genuine enthusiasm

## Chapter summary

- Referrals that are asked for deliberately happen far more often than referrals that are only hoped for; the discomfort of asking belongs to the seller, not the customer being asked.
- The best moment to ask is right after the customer has seen tangible proof of the result, not at the point of sale itself.
- A specific ask, aimed at a named person or a named kind of introduction, gets far more action than a vague, open-ended one.
- When there's no specific introduction available, a case study, testimonial, or review is still a specific, valuable ask.
- Closing the loop with an advocate, reporting back on what happened, is what turns one referral into a repeatable habit.
- Even a generous advocate can be over-asked; a genuine opening looks different from your own need for a new lead.

## Key takeaway

**A referral you asked for specifically is a system; a referral you merely hoped for is luck.**
