# Module 5: Understand the Problem

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Distinguish the symptom a buyer describes from the cause actually driving it
- Quantify the cost of a problem in the buyer's own numbers, not yours
- Confirm a diagnosis with the buyer before proposing anything, so you never solve the wrong problem well
- Recognise when the buyer's first description was actually correct, rather than manufacturing a deeper cause that isn't there
- Apply this to separate what a buyer asks for from what they actually need

## Why U follows L

Dominic told Priya, in their very first conversation, what the problem was: Bramble Analytics had outgrown its office. That statement is true, and it is also a symptom, not a diagnosis. A seller who accepts the buyer's own framing of the problem at face value will build a solution to that framing, competently, and still watch it fail to land, because the framing was never quite right to begin with. Learn the Customer gave Priya the access and the specifics to look past that first statement. Understand the Problem is where she actually does it.

Skip this stage, or rush through it, and you risk the failure mode that defines it: solving the wrong problem well. A beautifully designed larger office, delivered on time and on budget, can still be a loss if it wasn't what the business actually needed.

## Kestrel Office Interiors: what "we've outgrown this office" actually meant

Priya's next question to Dominic wasn't about square footage. "On a typical Tuesday, is the office full?" Dominic paused. "Actually, no. Tuesdays and Thursdays are packed, everyone else works from home the other three days." Priya asked what happened on the packed days. "It's chaos. No one can find a quiet room, people are on calls at their desks, and half the team ends up in a coffee shop down the street."

The problem wasn't that Bramble Analytics had too little space in total. It was that the space they had didn't flex to match wildly uneven daily attendance, so on the two busiest days it felt desperately cramped while sitting half-empty the rest of the week. A bigger office at the same fixed size would have solved the wrong problem, at a much higher cost than the one that actually needed solving.

## Separating the symptom from the cause

A symptom is what the buyer notices and says out loud, because it's the visible discomfort. A cause is the underlying condition producing that discomfort, and it's rarely stated in the first sentence because the buyer often hasn't traced it that far themselves; they've just been living with the symptom long enough to want it gone. "We've outgrown this office" was Dominic's honest description of what he felt. "Our space doesn't flex to match attendance that varies by 40 per cent between our busiest and quietest days" is the cause, and it points toward a completely different, and considerably cheaper, solution.

Getting from one to the other usually takes two or three specific follow-up questions, not a single clever one. Priya's question about a typical Tuesday worked because it was concrete and answerable in seconds, not because it was a trick.

Buyers describe symptoms first because a symptom is what they've actually experienced; a cause is an inference they usually haven't had a reason to work out. Dominic felt cramped on Tuesdays. He hadn't sat down and calculated that the real driver was an attendance pattern varying by 40 per cent, because doing that calculation wasn't his job. It was Priya's, once she had the access Module 4 gave her.

## When the first answer actually is the cause

Not every stated problem hides something deeper, and treating every buyer statement as automatically incomplete can create a diagnosis that isn't there. If Bramble Analytics genuinely needed more total desks because they'd hired fifteen people in three months with no corresponding attendance pattern to blame, "we've outgrown this office" would have been the complete, correct diagnosis, and further digging would only have wasted the buyer's patience. The test isn't whether you can find something underneath the first answer; it's whether the follow-up questions actually surface something that changes the shape of the solution. If Priya had asked about Tuesday attendance and Dominic had said "it's full every single day, evenly," that would have confirmed the original statement rather than complicated it, and the honest move would have been to accept it and move to Module 6.

## When there's more than one cause

Some problems don't resolve into a single clean cause. By the time Priya finished asking, Dominic's situation had at least two compounding factors: uneven attendance, and a noise problem that was itself discouraging attendance on the days it mattered most. These interacted rather than sitting side by side; solving only the space problem wouldn't have fixed the behavioural pattern it was partly causing. When a diagnosis surfaces more than one factor like this, resist the urge to pick the tidiest one and ignore the rest. Name all of them to the buyer, and let Module 6's value stack address each one separately rather than collapsing them into a single, oversimplified story.

## Quantify the cost, in their numbers

A vague problem produces a vague sense of urgency, and vague urgency is easy for a buyer to defer indefinitely. Priya asked Dominic to estimate how many people worked from a coffee shop on a typical packed day. "Maybe six or seven," he said. At a rough hourly cost of their time and the awkwardness of client calls taken in public, that put a real, specific number on a problem that had previously just been "annoying." Numbers the buyer generates themselves carry more weight internally than any figure you could offer them, because they can repeat those numbers to their own co-founder without having to defend where they came from.

## Confirm the diagnosis before proposing anything

Priya didn't move straight from that conversation into a proposal. She summarised it back to Dominic in one sentence: "So the real issue isn't total space, it's that your two busiest days need roughly twice the flexible capacity your quietest days do, and right now nobody has anywhere quiet to work when it's busy." Dominic agreed immediately, and added a detail Priya hadn't asked about: the noise wasn't just uncomfortable, it was making people less likely to come in on the days the business actually needed them there. Confirming the diagnosis out loud did two things: it caught an error before it became an expensive one, and it surfaced one more piece of the real cost.

A buyer's stated problem is usually a mix of four forces: the immediate irritation, the quantifiable cost of leaving it unsolved, the risk of getting a fix wrong, and what success would actually feel like. Missing any one of the four means an incomplete diagnosis.

## Apply this today

Take the problem your current prospect described to you in their own words. Ask one follow-up question specific enough to have a concrete, short answer (a day, a number, a moment it happens), the way Priya asked about a typical Tuesday. Write down how the answer changes, or doesn't change, what you thought the problem was.

## Measure success

You'll know this is working when you can summarise a buyer's problem back to them in a sentence they immediately agree with and then add detail to, rather than a sentence they have to correct. Corrections mean you're still solving the symptom; unprompted additions mean you've reached the actual cause.

## Common mistakes

- Accepting the buyer's first description of the problem as the finished diagnosis
- Proposing a solution before confirming the diagnosis out loud and getting agreement
- Leaving the cost of the problem vague instead of turning it into a number the buyer generated themselves
- Solving a bigger, more expensive version of the symptom instead of the smaller, cheaper cause
- Manufacturing a deeper cause that isn't actually there, when the buyer's first answer was already correct
- Picking the tidiest of several compounding causes and quietly ignoring the rest

## Chapter summary

- What a buyer says the problem is (the symptom) and what's actually causing it (the cause) are often different, and only the second one is worth solving.
- Buyers describe symptoms first because a symptom is what they've experienced; the cause is an inference that was never their job to work out.
- Sometimes the first answer is genuinely correct, and further digging only wastes the buyer's patience; the test is whether follow-up questions actually change the shape of the solution.
- Some problems have more than one compounding cause, and each one deserves its own place in the value case rather than being collapsed into a single story.
- Quantifying the cost in the buyer's own numbers creates urgency they can defend internally without your help.
- Confirming the diagnosis out loud before proposing anything catches mistakes early and often surfaces more of the real problem.

## Key takeaway

**The problem the buyer describes first is rarely the one worth solving.**
