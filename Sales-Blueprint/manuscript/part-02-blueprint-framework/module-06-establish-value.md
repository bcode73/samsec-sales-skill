# Module 6: Establish Value

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Distinguish value that is asserted from value that is demonstrated
- Translate a solution into the buyer's own language and numbers, not yours
- Build a value stack that itemises distinct outcomes so a price feels small against the pile
- Apply this to your own offer using a real diagnosed problem, not a generic pitch

## Why E follows U

Priya now knew, precisely, what was actually costing Bramble Analytics money and morale: six or seven people working from a coffee shop on peak days, and a growing reluctance among staff to come in when the business most needed them there. That diagnosis is what makes this stage possible. Establish Value means connecting a solution to that specific, already-confirmed problem, in terms Dominic himself supplied. Without Module 5's diagnosis, this stage collapses into generic claims about good design, the kind of value that gets asserted rather than demonstrated, and asserted value is exactly what buyers have learned to discount.

Skip straight to value without a confirmed diagnosis, and you end up presenting benefits that are true in general but not obviously connected to what this particular buyer told you was actually wrong.

## Kestrel Office Interiors: from design pitch to specific outcome

Priya's old approach would have shown Dominic a beautifully rendered floor plan and talked about flexible working culture. Instead, she opened with his own numbers. "You told me roughly six or seven people end up working from the coffee shop on your two busiest days. At even a conservative estimate of lost focus time and the client calls that shouldn't be taken in public, that's a real, recurring cost, and it's the thing this design solves directly." She then walked him through a layout with convertible focus rooms that expand on high-attendance days and contract into shared space the rest of the week, exactly matched to the attendance pattern he'd described, not a generic "flexible office" concept borrowed from a magazine.

Dominic's response was different from anything Priya had heard from him before. He didn't ask what it looked like. He asked how quickly it could be built.

## Assert less, demonstrate more

Asserted value is a claim: "our designs improve team productivity." It might be true, but it gives the buyer nothing to check it against, so it lands as marketing rather than evidence. Demonstrated value connects a specific feature of your solution to a specific outcome the buyer already told you they wanted, ideally using their own numbers. "This design gives you the equivalent of twelve additional focus-capable desks on your two busiest days, which is roughly the number of people you said end up working elsewhere" is a claim Dominic can verify against what he already knows to be true, because he's the one who supplied the six or seven in the first place.

This is also why value has to come after diagnosis and not before it. You cannot demonstrate a connection to a problem you haven't yet confirmed with the buyer.

## Speak the buyer's language, not your industry's

Priya doesn't talk to Dominic about "activity-based working" or "agile space utilisation," terms that mean something specific inside the office design industry and very little to an analytics company's operations lead. She talks about the coffee shop, the client calls, and the Tuesday and Thursday crunch, because those are the words Dominic used first. Jargon forces the buyer to translate your claim back into their own situation before they can evaluate it, and that translation step is exactly where interest quietly drops off.

## Build the value stack

A single benefit, however well demonstrated, is easy for a buyer to weigh against a single price and find wanting. A value stack lists every distinct outcome separately, so the buyer sees the full pile rather than one item. For Dominic, Priya's stack included the recovered focus time from staff no longer working off-site, the avoided cost of a full office expansion (a much larger and slower project than the one she was proposing), and a specific, if harder to quantify, improvement in in-office attendance on the days that mattered most to the business. Priced against any single one of those items, the project might look expensive. Priced against all three together, it looked like the obviously smaller cost.

## Apply this today

Take the diagnosed problem from Module 5's worksheet and write down every distinct outcome your solution produces for it, not just the headline one. Aim for at least three, and phrase each one using a number or a detail the buyer gave you, not a generic industry term.

## Measure success

You'll know this is working when a buyer's next question is about implementation or timing, not about what something means or how it's different from a competitor's offer. Questions about meaning signal the value hasn't landed yet; questions about logistics signal it has.

## Common mistakes

- Presenting value in industry language the buyer has to translate before they can evaluate it
- Asserting a benefit without connecting it to the specific problem the buyer already confirmed
- Offering one large, vague benefit instead of several distinct, itemised ones
- Building the value case before the diagnosis in Module 5 is actually confirmed

## Chapter summary

- Value that is asserted is a claim; value that is demonstrated is a connection the buyer can check against what they already told you.
- Speaking the buyer's own language, not your industry's, keeps them evaluating your claim instead of translating it.
- A value stack of several distinct, itemised outcomes makes a price look small by comparison; a single benefit rarely does.
- Establish Value only works once Understand the Problem has produced a diagnosis the buyer has already confirmed.

## Key takeaway

**Value isn't what you claim; it's what the buyer can check against what they already told you.**
