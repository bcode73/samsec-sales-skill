# Module 3: Build Trust

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Explain why Build Trust is the first stage of the Blueprint Framework and what breaks when it's skipped
- Distinguish genuine trust-building behaviour from charm, rapport, and small talk
- Diagnose the size of the trust deficit in a specific deal before you try to close it
- Apply three concrete, repeatable behaviours that build trust in the first conversation

## Why B comes first

Every stage after this one assumes the buyer is willing to take what you say at face value. Learn the Customer only works if they answer honestly. Establish Value only lands if they believe your numbers. Present the Offer only gets a fair hearing if they're not already looking for the catch. Build Trust exists to earn that willingness before you ask for it, which is why it is the first letter in the framework rather than an optional warm-up before the "real" pitch.

Skip it, and every later stage still happens, just filtered through suspicion. A prospect who doesn't trust you yet doesn't ignore your value proposition; they discount it, quietly, and you rarely find out by how much until the deal has already stalled.

## Kestrel Office Interiors: the deal Priya ran differently

A 45-person analytics company called Bramble Analytics was outgrowing its second office in two years, and their operations lead, a man named Dominic, had been burned before: the last fit-out ran three weeks over schedule with no warning. Priya's first meeting with him could easily have opened the way her pitches always had, with the portfolio and the walkthrough software. Instead, prompted by what she'd started noticing about her last few losses, she opened differently.

"Before I show you anything, can I ask what went wrong with the last company you used?" Dominic talked for ten minutes. Priya took notes and didn't interrupt once. At the end, she said, "I can't promise a project never runs late. What I can tell you is exactly how we'd flag it to you in week one if it started to, and here's the schedule format we'd use so you'd see it before we did." She hadn't shown him a single render yet.

## Trust is a signal, not a feeling

Buyers don't grant trust because they like you. They grant it because you've given them specific, checkable evidence that you understand their situation and won't hide bad news from them. That evidence comes in three forms, and a seller who leans on only one of them is building on a single leg.

**Competence signals** show you can actually do the work: relevant specifics about past jobs like this one, not a generic portfolio. **Reliability signals** show you'll do what you say, when you say it: Priya's schedule-flagging format was one, because it demonstrated a system, not a promise. **Candour signals** show you'll tell the buyer the truth even when it's not flattering to you: admitting a project could run late is a candour signal precisely because most sellers won't say it.

Charm and rapport can make a conversation pleasant without producing any of these three signals, which is why a likeable seller can still lose to a blunter one who actually answered the buyer's real question.

## The trust deficit is measurable, not vague

Every buyer starts a conversation with a certain amount of confidence they need before they'll commit, and a certain amount they actually have in you at that moment. The gap between those two is the trust deficit, and it is rarely the same size twice. Dominic's deficit was large and specific: he needed proof around schedule reliability more than proof of design skill, because that was where his last supplier had failed him. A seller who didn't ask his opening question would have spent the whole first meeting closing the wrong gap.

Before your next first meeting, you can estimate this deficit directly by asking what happened the last time this buyer tried to solve this problem, and listening for where it went wrong. That answer tells you which of the three signals to lead with.

Competence and reliability signals form the base; candour sits above them, because admitting a limitation only builds trust once the buyer already believes you're capable and dependable. Trust granted sits at the apex, not the start.

## Apply this today

Before your next first meeting, write down one question you can ask that surfaces what went wrong the last time this buyer tried to solve this problem. Ask it before you present anything. Then choose one candour signal, a real limitation of your product or service, and say it out loud in that same meeting, before the buyer has to discover it themselves.

## Measure success

You'll know this is working when a prospect starts volunteering information they wouldn't tell a stranger: a budget ceiling, an internal disagreement, a past bad experience. That kind of disclosure doesn't happen with someone the buyer is still filtering. Track it deal by deal; an increase in unprompted disclosure in first meetings is an early, leading signal that later-stage conversion will follow.

## Common mistakes

- Opening with your portfolio or credentials before asking anything about what the buyer has already tried
- Treating rapport (being liked) as a substitute for evidence (being trusted)
- Only offering competence signals when the buyer's actual deficit is about reliability or candour
- Hiding a real limitation instead of naming it, and having the buyer discover it later at a worse moment

## Chapter summary

- Build Trust comes first because every later stage assumes the buyer is willing to take what you say at face value.
- Trust is built from three specific signals: competence, reliability, and candour, not from charm or rapport.
- Every buyer has a trust deficit of a particular size and shape, and you can estimate it by asking what went wrong last time.
- Candour, admitting a real limitation, only builds trust once competence and reliability are already established.

## Key takeaway

**Trust is not something you ask for; it's something you demonstrate before you ask for anything else.**
