# Module 10: Nurture Relationships

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Explain why the sale closing is the start of the relationship's value, not the end of it
- Build a scheduled, deliberate contact cadence rather than an occasional reactive one
- Distinguish genuine nurture from a disguised sales pitch
- Respond well when a nurture check-in surfaces a problem rather than good news
- Scale a nurture cadence across many customers without it turning mechanical
- Apply this to a customer you closed weeks or months ago and haven't spoken to since

## Why N follows I

The signed agreement in Module 9 was Kestrel's biggest achievement in the deal so far, and it's also the point where most sellers, without quite meaning to, stop treating the relationship as something to actively manage. Priya's old pattern matched that exactly: deliver the project, invoice it, and only reappear if something went wrong or if there was something new to sell. Nurture Relationships exists because the value of a customer doesn't end at delivery; in many businesses, it barely begins there.

Skip this stage, and you get the failure mode that defines it: contact stops the day the invoice is paid, and a relationship that could have compounded into repeat business and referrals quietly goes cold instead.

## Kestrel Office Interiors: the call that wasn't a pitch

The Bramble Analytics installation went in over a long weekend, exactly as scheduled around Tuesdays and Thursdays. Six weeks later, Priya called Dominic, not with an invoice query or an upsell, but with one question: "Are people still ending up at the coffee shop on your busy days?" Dominic laughed. "Barely. Actually, a couple of other teams have asked if they can book the flexible rooms for their own busy days now too."

That call produced no revenue and pitched nothing. It also told Priya something she wouldn't have learned otherwise: the flexible space was being used more widely across Bramble Analytics than the original brief covered, which meant the relationship had room to grow on its own, without Priya having to manufacture a reason to reach out.

## When the check-in surfaces a problem instead

Dominic's answer happened to be good news, but the same question could easily have surfaced a problem instead, and that outcome is still worth having asked for. If the flexible rooms had turned out not to be used the way Priya designed them, hearing that at six weeks, directly and early, is far better than discovering it a year later from a client who's quietly decided not to work with Kestrel again. A nurture check-in that surfaces a real problem is an opportunity to apply the same candour signal from Module 3: acknowledge it, propose a specific fix, and treat the honesty of the conversation as part of what the relationship is actually worth. Sellers who only check in when they expect good news are, without realising it, avoiding exactly the conversations most likely to save a relationship that's quietly going wrong.

## Nurture is scheduled, not reactive

A relationship that only gets attention when something breaks or when there's a new proposal to send teaches the customer, correctly, that contact from you means either a problem or a pitch. Priya built a simple cadence instead: a check-in at six weeks, purely about how the space was performing against the original problem; a check-in at six months, about anything that had changed in the business since; and an open, no-agenda note around the anniversary of the project, sharing something genuinely useful (an article, an observation from another client's similar situation) rather than a reason to buy again.

## The nurture cadence, at a glance

| Touchpoint | Timing | Purpose | What it isn't |
|---|---|---|---|
| First check-in | Six weeks after delivery | Confirm the original diagnosis actually held up in practice | An upsell attempt |
| Business check-in | Six months after delivery | Learn what's changed in the business since | A renewal pitch |
| Anniversary note | Around twelve months | Share something genuinely useful | A reason to buy again |

## Genuine nurture has no pitch hiding in it

The test for whether a follow-up is nurture or a disguised pitch is simple: if you removed any possibility of future revenue from the conversation, would you still make the call? Priya's six-week question about the coffee shop passed that test easily; she wanted to know whether the diagnosis in Module 5 had actually held up in practice, independent of whether Bramble Analytics ever bought from her again. A customer can tell the difference between a call like that and a call that's really a warmed-up sales pitch wearing a check-in's clothes, and the difference is exactly what determines whether they pick up the phone next time.

This is also why genuine nurture builds more goodwill than more frequent transactional contact: a gesture with no attached ask is legible as generosity, while the same gesture immediately followed by a pitch reads, correctly, as a means to an end. Buyers remember which sellers gave them something for nothing far more clearly than they remember any single feature of what was sold to them.

## Scaling nurture without making it mechanical

A handful of customers is easy to nurture individually; a growing client base is not, and the temptation is to templatise every message until it reads like the automated marketing emails this whole approach is meant to avoid. The way to keep it genuine at scale is to keep the schedule systematic (the timing of six weeks, six months, an anniversary can absolutely be a calendar reminder) while keeping the content specific to that customer every time. A reminder that says "call Bramble Analytics about the flexible rooms" is a system. A message that opens with "are people still ending up at the coffee shop" is what makes the system feel, correctly, like it isn't one. Module 13 covers building this into a repeatable process across an entire pipeline; the discipline that keeps it from feeling automated starts here, in what you actually say once the reminder fires.

## Apply this today

Pick one customer you closed more than a month ago and haven't spoken to since. Call or message them with a single, genuine question about how what you delivered is actually working, with nothing to sell attached. Note what they tell you; it's often information you can't get any other way.

## Put the cadence on a calendar, not in your memory

Rather than relying on remembering to check in, set the six-week, six-month, and anniversary touchpoints as calendar reminders the moment a deal closes, for every customer, not just the ones you happen to think of. When a reminder fires, spend a minute recalling something specific to that customer before you reach out, so the message still reads as genuine rather than templated. The schedule can run on autopilot; the content never should.

## Measure success

You'll know this is working when past customers respond quickly and openly to your check-ins, rather than warily, and when some of those conversations surface expansion opportunities or referrals without you having asked for either. A growing list of customers you're in genuine, non-transactional contact with, months after the invoice was paid, is the concrete sign this stage is working.

## Common mistakes

- Going silent after delivery until a problem or a new pitch gives you a reason to call
- Disguising a sales pitch as a check-in, which customers usually recognise immediately
- Treating nurture as occasional and reactive instead of scheduled and deliberate
- Assuming a satisfied customer will stay engaged without any active effort from you
- Only checking in when you expect good news, and avoiding the calls most likely to catch a problem early
- Templatising the content of a check-in so heavily that it stops feeling specific to that customer

## Chapter summary

- The value of a customer relationship often only begins at the point most sellers stop paying attention to it.
- A scheduled cadence of genuine check-ins beats reactive contact that only happens around problems or pitches.
- A follow-up that would still be worth making even with no possible future sale attached is genuine nurture; one that wouldn't is a disguised pitch.
- A check-in that surfaces a problem is still a success; it catches issues early instead of losing the customer quietly later.
- Nurture surfaces information and opportunities (expansion, referral) that you can't get any other way, precisely because it isn't asking for anything.
- The schedule can scale systematically across a whole client base; the content of each message has to stay specific to that customer or the system starts to show.

## Key takeaway

**The invoice being paid is the start of the relationship's value, not the end of the transaction that produced it.**
